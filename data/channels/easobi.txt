Chair Kevin Warsh said policymakers had “removed a dose of accommodation,” adding that broad financial conditions were difficult to describe as restrictive. He emphasized that economic activity was “expanding at a solid pace,” and said recent inflation readings had not shown a meaningful improvement in underlying trends. The Fed said the increase would support a “timelier return” of inflation to its 2% goal, while its updated projections pointed to further tightening.

But we do not expect a measured Fed tightening cycle to derail the equity rally.

Much of the tightening is already priced. There was a hawkish tone to the meeting—both at the press conference and the economic projections. Sixteen of the 18 participants expect at least one more increase this year, compared with only half in June. Warsh pointed to the breadth of inflation, with too many categories rising at annualized rates of 3% or more over both six- and 12-month periods. But markets entered the meeting bracing for nearly four increases over the cycle, compared with our forecast for two. Treasury yields rose, with the 2-year yield rising 13bps in the immediate aftermath of the decision, while equities moved lower as investors absorbed the announcement and press conference. However, both moves were relatively contained by Wednesday's close, with the S&P 500 down 0.4% and the 2-year yield up just 1bp on the day. With the first hike now delivered, we continue to expect one further 25-basis-point increase this year.

Economic strength makes tightening more manageable. The Fed raised its median growth forecasts for 2026 and 2027 to 2.3% and 2.4%, from 2.2% and 2.3% in June, while lowering the projected unemployment rate for both years to 4.1% from 4.3%. This was reinforced earlier in the day by the August retail sales figures, which painted a picture of resilient demand, rising 1.2% from the prior month, above expectations for 0.8%, with gains in 12 of the 13 categories. This suggests the economy has room to absorb measured tightening. Core PCE inflation is projected at 3.4% in 2026 and is expected to return to the Fed’s 2% target only in 2029. Continued AI investment provides additional support, and we forecast AI industry capital spending to rise by around one-third, from USD 900bn in 2026 to USD 1.2tr in 2027.

Strong earnings can counter higher yields. Warsh pointed to economic strength, competition for capital as AI hyperscalers raise funding, and geopolitical tensions as factors affecting long-term yields. This suggests the rise in yields reflects more than expectations for Fed policy alone. Higher discount rates can weigh on equity valuations, but stronger growth and investment can also support profits. We forecast S&P 500 earnings growth of 25% in 2026 and 14% in 2027. A more difficult environment for equities would require not only higher yields, but also weaker earnings expectations and wider credit spreads.

So, we remain positioned for further equity gains while preparing for near-term volatility.
