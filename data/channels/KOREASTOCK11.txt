[2026-11-19 09:19] AI stocks are in a 'Tesla-style lull,' not a bubble burst, says Seoul strategist

Key Points

• AI and chip stocks keep sliding despite Nvidia's blowout quarter and upbeat guidance.

• Seoul-based strategist Simon Choi says the market has entered a "Tesla-style lull," a pause before the next leg higher.

• He sees a repeat of the late 1990s: productivity keeps rates in check, followed by a genuine bubble.

AI stocks are getting hammered, and it isn't for lack of good news.

Nvidia posted third-quarter revenue of $115.8 billion this week, beating Wall Street estimates by more than $6 billion, and guided to $130 billion for the current quarter. Anthropic's IPO priced at $83 a share. Data center permits are still clearing in Texas.

The stocks haven't cared. Nvidia is down roughly 24% this month. The PHLX Semiconductor Index logged its worst day since March 2020 on Nov. 5, and the selling has rippled through Seoul, Taipei and Tokyo.

Simon Choi, an equity investment specialist and founder of Seoul-based BIGSHOT RESEARCH, says investors are misreading the moment.

"This is a Tesla-style lull," Choi said. "The market is resting, not reversing."

His argument: AI's productivity gains are real, but most people can't see them yet. They haven't shown up in the economic data, and they haven't triggered the disinflation that would give the Federal Reserve cover to cut rates. Until that happens, high rates will keep a lid on growth stocks, no matter how strong the earnings.

Tesla's playbook

Choi points to Tesla in 2018 as the template.

Demand for the Model 3 was overwhelming, but the company couldn't build cars fast enough. Elon Musk called it "production hell," and later admitted Tesla came within weeks of going bankrupt. Musk himself became a liability: his "funding secured" tweet triggered an SEC lawsuit and cost him his chairman's seat.

The stock went sideways for the better part of two years. The story never broke.

"Tesla almost went under too," Choi said. "People who saw that as a lull, not the end, made a fortune."

The parallels are striking. Today, as then, demand isn't the problem; capacity is. And the industry's bellwether is weathering a CEO shock at the worst possible time, after Jensen Huang's abrupt resignation from Nvidia.

Back to 1997

For what comes after the lull, Choi looks further back, to the early internet era.

In March 1997, the Fed raised rates by a quarter point to 5.5%, its first hike in two years. Many on Wall Street braced for a long tightening cycle. It never came. Productivity gains from the internet and the PC kept inflation unusually low, and that single move proved to be the only hike of the cycle. Within 18 months, the Fed was cutting.

Choi expects the same script this time. The productivity payoff from AI hasn't shown up yet, he says, but it will surface as the market moves through the lull.

"The Fed might hike once more, maybe twice," Choi said. "But that will be the last of it."

If he's right, the upside could be dramatic. In South Korea, the tech-heavy KOSDAQ rose from about 60 in October 1998 to 283 by March 2000, a nearly fivefold gain in 17 months. Its leaders did far better. Saerom Technology, the internet-phone darling of the day, soared from 1,890 won in October 1999 to 282,000 won five months later, a roughly 150-fold jump. Dozens of others rose 10- to 20-fold.

"We could see a real bubble," Choi said. "Not the one people are worried about now, but the kind we saw in 1998 and 1999."

What ends the lull

Choi says the catalyst will be data, not earnings. Once AI's productivity gains start showing up in the numbers and inflation cools, the case for rate cuts makes itself, and the sell-off will look the way Tesla's 2018 slump looks now.

South Korea, he says, is where the gap is widest. Korean memory makers have much of their supply already locked in under contract, so profits will keep rolling in during the lull. The KOSPI trades at just 3.4 times forward earnings, a record low.

"The earnings are already there," Choi said. "The price just hasn't caught up."
